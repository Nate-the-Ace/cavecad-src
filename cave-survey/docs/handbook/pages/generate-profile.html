Generate Profile

Rebuild the elevation beside the plan — extended or projected — with floor and ceiling lines from LRUD and splays. gp

When you reach for it

To change how the elevation is drawn, or to force a rebuild from the drawing's own tags and see what it could not show. An extended elevation happens on its own with every draw; this is where you choose otherwise.

Steps

  1. Cave Survey > Generate Profile.
  2. Pick the elevation you want — extended, projected, or chunked. The choice is remembered, so every later draw keeps drawing that kind.
  3. Read the report: a run it could not band is named rather than skipped silently.

Which elevation

A cave has one elevation and two ways of drawing it. Which one is right is a property of the cave.

Extended unrolls the passage: the horizontal axis is distance travelled, so no passage hides behind another and every leg draws at its true length. A long wandering horizontal cave can be read end to end. What it cannot show is where anything actually is — two chambers a hundred feet apart underground can end up side by side on the page.

Projected flattens the real coordinates onto one compass line. Passages do hide behind each other and a leg running across the plane draws short; in exchange the drawing is the cave, at true relative position and true depth. Two shafts one above the other are drawn one above the other.

For a pit, take the projected one. The arrangement of the shafts is the cave — unroll it and you have thrown away the only thing worth drawing.

Leave the plane blank and the cave's own longest direction is used, which is the plane that throws away least: for a cave following a joint that is the joint, for one following a stream the stream. The caption says which way you are looking, and says when the plane was chosen for you rather than by you. A cave with no horizontal extent at all — a single shaft — has no preferred plane, and the caption says that too.

040 and 220 are the same plane. An axis is a line, not a direction.

Chunked cuts the cave into pieces at its pitches and lays them out side by side. A drop is one piece, rebelays included; the passage between two drops is another. Each piece is flattened onto its own plane, which is most of why the pieces read better than the whole cave does on one.

Nothing is displaced. A piece slides sideways and never up or down, so every piece on the sheet sits at its real elevation and any two depths on the drawing compare by eye. That is the thing the extended elevation gives up when it pushes a band below true depth and writes a caption explaining itself.

Dotted lines join the stations two pieces share — a real correspondence, the same station drawn in both, not a decorative arrow. They are the only lines on the drawing that are not passage.

Drag a piece and it stays dragged. The first draw puts the pieces in a row so there is something on the page; move one and its position is written onto the drawing beside it, so a redraw after the survey gains a station puts everything back where you left it. Every piece carries a position, including the ones you have not touched — otherwise a redraw between two drags would reflow the pieces that had not moved.

Each piece is drawn the way its own shape deserves, and nothing anywhere declares what kind of cave you have. A piece is measured: if flattening it onto a plane would fold it back behind itself — a trunk that wanders, so parts of it hide behind the parts coming the other way — that piece is unrolled instead and reads end to end. Everything else stays projected, at true position. Pitfall Cave's main trunk walks 1424 ft along its plane in 825 ft of page, so it unrolls; every rope in Plumbline Pit folds by nothing, so none of them do. Same engine, opposite answers, no setting.

Unrolling needs one path through a piece, so a branch off a trunk would be dropped. It isn't: the branch becomes a piece of its own, tied back at the junction it leaves, at its own true depth. Nothing is lost to the choice.

Take this one when a cave has both kinds of passage in it — which most caves do.

Pitches

Every drop the band draws is labelled beside the middle of its line — P 187 ft (62 + 125). In the plan the number exists because a pitch has no size at all; here it exists for the opposite reason. The drop is the most prominent thing on the page and the one line with no scale cue of its own: no walls either side, no bends, nothing but two station points at its ends.

The text is the same text the plan draws, from the same place, so the two views cannot disagree about how deep a drop is.

A drop the band does not draw whole gets no number. A run with two arms keeps the longer one, so a pitch down the shorter arm has its bottom nowhere on the page — and half a line with a depth beside it would be a claim about geometry the reader cannot see. The plan still labels it: the plan never splits a cave into bands.
